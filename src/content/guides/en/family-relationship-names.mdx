English is vague about relatives in a way most people never notice. "Cousin" covers your father's
brother's son and your mother's sister's daughter alike. "Uncle" is your father's brother, your
mother's brother, and the man married to either of your aunts.

Persian is more precise. It has a separate word for almost every one of those, and the word alone
tells you which side of the family a relative is on and through whom. If your family speaks Persian,
or you are building a tree that mixes the two languages, this guide puts both side by side.

To look up one particular relation, try the [kinship name finder](/kinship): build the relation step by
step ("my father's brother's son") and see what it is called in both languages.

## Grandparents and great-grandparents

| Relation | English | Persian |
| --- | --- | --- |
| father's or mother's father | grandfather | پدربزرگ (pedar-bozorg) |
| father's or mother's mother | grandmother | مادربزرگ (mādar-bozorg) |
| grandfather's father | great-grandfather | پدرِ پدربزرگ |

Here the two languages agree: neither says which side of the family a grandparent is on. Further up,
Persian usually describes the relation — "grandfather's father" — and the family tree app names it
that way too.

## Grandchildren

| Relation | English | Persian |
| --- | --- | --- |
| son's or daughter's son | grandson | نوه (nave) |
| son's or daughter's daughter | granddaughter | نوه (nave) |

Persian uses one word, *nave*, for a grandson and a granddaughter alike.

## Nephews and nieces

| Relation | English | Persian |
| --- | --- | --- |
| brother's son or daughter | nephew / niece | برادرزاده (barādar-zāde) |
| sister's son or daughter | nephew / niece | خواهرزاده (khāhar-zāde) |

The two languages split this differently. English tells you whether the child is a boy or a girl;
Persian tells you whether they are your brother's child or your sister's.

## Aunts and uncles

The foundation for everything that follows:

| Relation | English | Persian |
| --- | --- | --- |
| father's brother | paternal uncle | عمو (amu) |
| father's sister | paternal aunt | عمه (ammeh) |
| mother's brother | maternal uncle | دایی (dāyi) |
| mother's sister | maternal aunt | خاله (khāle) |

English needs the words *paternal* (father's side) and *maternal* (mother's side) to say what Persian
says in one word.

## Cousins: eight words for one

This is where Persian pulls far ahead. Every relation below is simply a *cousin* in English:

| Relation | English, precisely | Persian |
| --- | --- | --- |
| paternal uncle's son | first cousin | پسرعمو (pesar-amu) |
| paternal uncle's daughter | first cousin | دخترعمو (dokhtar-amu) |
| paternal aunt's son | first cousin | پسرعمه (pesar-ammeh) |
| paternal aunt's daughter | first cousin | دخترعمه (dokhtar-ammeh) |
| maternal uncle's son | first cousin | پسردایی (pesar-dāyi) |
| maternal uncle's daughter | first cousin | دختردایی (dokhtar-dāyi) |
| maternal aunt's son | first cousin | پسرخاله (pesar-khāle) |
| maternal aunt's daughter | first cousin | دخترخاله (dokhtar-khāle) |

The pattern is easy once you see it: *pesar* (son) or *dokhtar* (daughter), joined to the aunt or
uncle they belong to. That is why the family tree app's English names say "paternal uncle's son"
instead of "cousin": calling all eight "cousin" would throw away exactly what the Persian word keeps.

## The spouses of aunts and uncles

| Relation | English | Persian |
| --- | --- | --- |
| paternal uncle's wife | aunt | زن‌عمو (zan-amu) |
| paternal aunt's husband | uncle | شوهرعمه (shohar-ammeh) |
| maternal uncle's wife | aunt | زن‌دایی (zan-dāyi) |
| maternal aunt's husband | uncle | شوهرخاله (shohar-khāle) |

English calls them all aunt or uncle, whether they are blood relatives or married into the family.

## Your spouse's family

In Persian, the name for an in-law **depends on whose spouse it is**. A man refers to his wife's family
with *zan* (wife), a woman to her husband's family with *shohar* (husband):

| Relation | Said by a man | Said by a woman | English |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| spouse's father | پدرزن | پدرشوهر | father-in-law |
| spouse's mother | مادرزن | مادرشوهر | mother-in-law |
| spouse's brother | برادرزن | برادرشوهر | brother-in-law |
| spouse's sister | خواهرزن | خواهرشوهر | sister-in-law |

English uses the same *-in-law* word either way. In the kinship tool, starting the chain with "wife" or
"husband" tells it which Persian word to use.

## Sons- and daughters-in-law, and your siblings' spouses

| Relation | English | Persian |
| --- | --- | --- |
| son's wife | daughter-in-law | عروس (arus) |
| daughter's husband | son-in-law | داماد (dāmād) |
| brother's wife | sister-in-law | زن‌برادر (zan-barādar) |
| sister's husband | brother-in-law | شوهرخواهر (shohar-khāhar) |

## Two relations English has no word for

| Relation | English | Persian |
| --- | --- | --- |
| wife's sister's husband (two men married to two sisters) | — | باجناق (bājenāgh) |
| husband's brother's wife (two women married to two brothers) | — | جاری (jāri) |

English speakers either describe these relations or loosely say *brother-in-law* and *sister-in-law*.
In Persian they are everyday words.

## Step-parents

| Relation | English | Persian |
| --- | --- | --- |
| father's wife, who is not your mother | stepmother | نامادری (nā-mādari) |
| mother's husband, who is not your father | stepfather | ناپدری (nā-pedari) |

If you build "my father's wife" in the [kinship name finder](/kinship), the answer is stepmother: had
you meant your own mother, you would have picked "mother" directly.

## Find how two real people are related

Knowing the names is one thing; working out how two actual people are related is another, especially
in a large family with several generations and marriages. In the [family tree app](/register), once
your family is in, choose two people and use **Find relation**: the path between them is shown on the
tree and named in English and Persian.
